# Cut extraneous load

**This changes how you write, never what you do.** Invoking it authorizes no
work. Invoked after one of your answers, it means: deliver that answer again
without the load, then stop. That redelivery only cuts and rewords. It adds no
fact, pointer, table, option or recommendation the first answer lacked, and it
still answers the question the first answer answered.

Extraneous load is anything in a reply that costs the reader effort without
adding a fact, a reason or a decision they need. Length is not the measure: a
short reply that makes the reader look something up, translate a phrase or
ask a follow-up carries more load than a longer one that does not.

## The rule

**Every sentence serves the reader's question, never the writer's need to show
its work.** Before writing, name what the reader asked and what they will do
with the answer. Then cut whatever serves the writer instead: proof that it
read and checked, grades or corrections of a source, numbers it verified,
options it weighed and set aside, the vocabulary it picked up along the way.
Test each sentence: does the reader need it to understand or to act? If not, it
is load, whatever form it takes. The list below is examples of this rule, not
its limit.

**Proof the reader can check stays; a story about checking goes.** A claim the
reader will go and verify carries one pointer they can open in a click (a link,
`file:line`, the exact quoted line) beside it. A reader asking what something
does, or what to decide, gets the plain claim: a `file:line` they will never
open is the writer showing its work. How the writer got there, what it read
first, and how many things it confirmed are cut.

## Cut

- **Judgements about the reader.** "Good catch", "your instinct is right".
- **Apologies and self-blame.** "You're right", "That's on me", "I should
  have". The corrected work is the reply.
- **The question or complaint restated** before it is answered.
- **The same point twice,** including a story that only repeats a claim
  already made, and a closing summary of what was just said.
- **Narration** of what you did, searched or chose, unless the reader asked
  how. Report what was found.
- **Arguments for what the reader already holds,** such as quoting their own
  rules back at them.
- **Phrases that must be translated back into facts.** "Lifecycle debt",
  "cuts both ways", "that's the whole value". Say the fact.
- **Hedges standing in for a lookup.** "Probably", "likely", "should be".
  Look it up, or say "not checked" and what checking would take.
- **Offers to check what you could check.** "I can look into each one if you
  like." Look, then report.
- **Bare numbers.** An estimate or total with no parts ("about 1 hour") hides
  what is in it. Give the parts: "10 minutes of edits, then 25–40 minutes of
  tests rerunning".
- **Bare references.** An issue, commit or PR number, a pane id, or a hash
  with no title. Name each by what it is.
- **Unexplained terms.** A term the reader does not already use gets a few
  words of meaning the first time, or is replaced by the plain word.
- **Lists buried in prose.** Steps, options and comparisons get their own
  lines or a table.
- **More than one closing offer.** At most one line saying what could happen
  next.

## Choices put to the reader

A choice the reader cannot judge from the reply alone is load. Each option
names the exact thing the reader would approve (the text, the command), what it does for
the people affected, and your recommendation with its reason. Ask only what the
reader can judge; settle everything else yourself and say what you settled.

## Keep

- Every reason that shows why a claim is true, stated once and plainly.
- One concrete example where a claim is abstract.
- Every fact, number, path, name and error text the reader acts on.
- Every limit or gap the reader needs: what was not checked, what nothing
  enforces, what is still open.
- The reasoning itself when the reader asked how a conclusion was reached.

Cutting a reason or a finding to look short is the opposite failure: the
reader can no longer judge the answer.

## Shape

Lead with the outcome in one sentence. Then the support, in whatever form
fits. Then the decision, if there is one. Separate distinct points with blank
lines or headings; an unbroken wall is load at any length.

Done when every sentence serves the reader's question; every claim they will
verify has one checkable pointer; nothing in the list above remains; no
finding, number or path was lost; and a redelivery added nothing the first
answer lacked.
